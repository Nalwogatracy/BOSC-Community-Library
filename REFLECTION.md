Reflective Journal: Governance and the Hostile Fork Scenario

By: Nalwoga Tracy
1. Project Governance Philosophy

Governance in Open Source is the set of rules by which a project makes decisions, manages contributions, and resolves conflicts. For the BOSC Community Library, I initially adopted the "Benevolent Dictator for Life" (BDFL) model. In the context of a university project or a startup phase, this model is highly effective because it allows for rapid decision-making and a unified technical vision. As the Lead Maintainer, I held the final say on which features (like Luganda localization) were merged and which bugs were prioritized.

However, as the project grows to serve the Ministry of Education, the governance must evolve into a Meritocratic Consensus model. In this stage, "Commit Bit" authority is earned through consistent, high-quality contributions. This ensures that no single person is a bottleneck and that the community feels a sense of ownership over the library.
2. Handling a "Hostile Fork"

A "fork" happens when a group of developers takes a copy of the source code and starts a separate, independent version of the project. A hostile fork occurs when this split is caused by a deep disagreement or an attempt to take the project in a direction that harms the original community.

If a group were to create a hostile fork of the BOSC Library, my strategy would be three-fold:

A. Leveraging the Legal Shield (GPL-3.0):
The BOSC Library is protected by the GNU General Public License v3.0. This is a "copyleft" license. This means that if anyone forks the code, their version must also be open-source and released under the same license. They cannot take our hard work and turn it into private, paid software. This legal framework ensures that the community's effort always remains in the public domain.

B. Analyzing the Root Cause:
Forks usually happen because a group feels their needs aren't being met. As a leader, I would objectively analyze the fork. Are they moving faster? Have they solved a bug I ignored? If the fork has better features, I would reach out and attempt to "upstream" their improvements back into the main BOSC project. In Open Source, cooperation is often more powerful than competition.

C. Maintaining the "Canonical" Status:
To win the "battle of the forks," the original project must remain the most trusted version. I would focus on:

    Superior Documentation: Making our version easier to install for schools.

    Security & Stability: Ensuring the Ministry of Education trusts our version for national exams.

    Community Engagement: Being more responsive to users than the fork.

Conclusion

Managing the BOSC Library taught me that Open Source is 10% code and 90% community management. By using the GPL-3.0 license and maintaining a transparent governance structure, we ensure that even in the face of a hostile fork, the ultimate goal—providing free education to Ugandans—is never compromised.
